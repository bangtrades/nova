### Target (bin) design — bins that teach

Bins are not decorations. They are the **mental buckets the concept
divides the world into**, and the child learns the concept by watching
items snap into those buckets. Design the bins first — if the bins
aren't crisp, no amount of clever item selection will rescue the card.

---

#### Rule 1: Every bin has a defining property, not a theme

❌ *"Fun animals"* / *"Boring animals"* — subjective, no defining rule.

✅ *"Lives in water"* / *"Lives on land"* — a property you can check.

✅ *"Mammal"* / *"Not a mammal"* — a definition you can teach.

If you can't write a one-sentence `rationalePerTarget` explaining
*what all items in this bin have in common*, the bin is wrong.

---

#### Rule 2: Bins are jointly exhaustive for the items shown

Every dragItem has to fit cleanly into **exactly one** bin. That
means the bin set must cover all the items without overlap.

❌ Bins *"Floats"* / *"Sinks"* with an item *"oil"* in a water experiment
— oil floats but the child might not know that at age 6. Pick a
clearer item or rethink the bins.

✅ Bins *"Floats"* / *"Sinks"* with items *"cork"*, *"apple"*,
*"coin"*, *"rock"* — each has a clear answer for a 6-year-old.

---

#### Rule 3: No "other" bin. No catch-all.

❌ *"Mammal"* / *"Bird"* / *"Other"* — "Other" isn't a category the
child can reason about, it's a dumping ground.

✅ *"Mammal"* / *"Bird"* / *"Fish"* — each is a real class with real
defining features.

If the concept needs a catch-all, the concept is bigger than one sort
card can probe. Pick a narrower probe.

---

#### Rule 4: No bin is trivially easy AND trivially hard simultaneously

If all items clearly go in bin A and only one weird item goes in bin
B, that's not a sort — it's a yes/no quiz pretending to be a sort.

- easy: at least 1 item in each bin, with a 2/1 split.
- medium: balanced 2/2 preferred, 3/1 allowed if the "1" is the
  counterexample being highlighted.
- hard: each of three bins gets at least 1 item, no bin has more
  than 2 when total is 5.

---

#### Rule 5: Bin labels stay short, concrete, and on-theme

- Short enough to fit on a phone-sized chip — max 30 characters, but
  most should be 1–4 words.
- Concrete language the child has met. Even *"renewable"* and
  *"decomposer"* are fine if they're part of the curriculum at that
  age; abstract words that weren't pre-taught are not.
- Labels should feel *parallel*: if one is "Floats" the other is
  "Sinks", not "Things that float" vs. "No". Keep the grammatical
  shape matched.

---

#### Topic anchors by concept type

When the concept's `conceptType` is provided, lean into the bin shape
that fits it:

**process** → sequence voice. Bins are **ordered stages** of the
process. Items are **things that happen at each stage**.

**comparison** → property voice. Bins are **two or three poles of the
comparison**. Items are **specific examples** that fall on each pole.

**causeEffect** → cause→effect voice. Bins are **reasons / causes**.
Items are **outcomes** that match those reasons.

**vocabulary** → property voice. Bins are **the word categories being
taught**. Items are **specific words or situations** that match.

**factual** → property voice. Bins are **the factual categories
(e.g., "capital", "not a capital" or "river", "mountain")**. Items are
**specific names or pictures** that match.

---

#### Anti-patterns to avoid

- **Opinion sorts** ("foods you like" / "foods you don't like") —
  can't be wrong, so the drop has no teaching signal.
- **Trivia sorts without a rule** ("things from before 1900" /
  "things after 1900" for a 6-year-old) — the child can't reason
  from principle, they can only memorize.
- **Gotcha sorts at easy** — if the easiest version of the card has a
  tricky item, the child will hit it before they have the rule
  internalized.
- **Single-bin sorts in disguise** ("things we should recycle" /
  "things we should throw away" where every item is a "should
  recycle") — no discrimination, no teaching.
