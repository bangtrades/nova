### Difficulty: medium — 4 items × 2 bins

A sort with a twist. The two categories are still mutually exclusive,
but at least one item now has a **secondary property** that could
mislead a child who only half-grasped the concept. Sorting forces them
to lean on the definition rather than surface features.

**Counts**
- `dragItems.length` = **4**
- `dropTargets.length` = **2**
- Distribution: balanced where possible (2/2), at minimum 3/1. Never
  4/0.

**Cognitive load**
- Application-level. The child must *apply* the rule, not just
  recognize the prototype. Expect 3–6 seconds per item.
- Exactly **one** item is a mild counterexample — looks like it might
  belong in bin A at first glance, actually belongs in bin B. The
  other three are clean.
- Examples of counterexamples done right: *whale* in mammals (looks
  like a fish), *bat* in mammals (looks like a bird), *tomato* under
  fruit (looks like a vegetable), *ice cube* as solid water (looks
  very different from liquid water).

**Bin design for medium**
- Same two-bin structure as easy, but the rule is named in the
  instructions so the child has something to apply. *"Mammals feed
  milk to their babies. Sort each animal into the right group."*
- Target labels are 1–3 words, often a single domain term.

**Item design for medium**
- Three prototypical items + one mild counterexample.
- Each item still has exactly one correct bin — the counterexample is
  tricky but not genuinely ambiguous.
- Counterexamples should be cases where a good teacher would say
  *"ahh, see, this is WHY the definition matters."*
