### Age 8 — fluent reader, multi-strand lesson with synthesis

At eight, the child reads fluently, holds a five-minute argument in
their head, and reasons about *why* a concept works — not just
*that* it does. The decomposition can carry **5–6 atoms**, weave in
causal chains, and include a synthesis atom where two earlier atoms
converge. This is also the age where **voice** becomes a tool you
can reach for sparingly — a spoken recall atom near the end works
well for vocabulary- and language-heavy topics.

**Sequence shape at age 8**

- Open with a **narrative** atom OR a **comparison** atom (an
  eight-year-old will bite on "here are two things that look the
  same but are different"). If the topic has a strong story, use
  narrative; if the topic is comparative by nature (mammals vs.
  birds, solid vs. liquid, renewable vs. nonrenewable), comparison
  opens fine.
- Middle atoms (3–4 of them) mix **explanation**, **comparison**,
  **cause_effect**, and **experiment**. Cause-effect earns its keep
  here — the child can finally hold "X caused Y" as a claim worth
  examining.
- Penultimate atom: either an **experiment** (transfer-level sort)
  or, if the topic is spoken/linguistic, a **voice** atom (sparingly,
  never more than one per lesson).
- Close with a **quiz** atom (4–5 options, transfer-level — at
  least one distractor must probe a real misconception).

**Atom descriptions**

- 40–280 characters each. Up to 2 sentences. Can include compound
  claims and a because-clause: *"A leaf is green because it holds
  chlorophyll, a pigment that captures sunlight to make the plant's
  food."*
- Domain vocabulary is welcome if an earlier atom taught or if the
  description glosses it inline. Don't chain undefined jargon.
- Use the because-form freely. An eight-year-old learns more from a
  "because" than from a "therefore".

**Prerequisites**

- First atom: zero prerequisites (or one prerequisite from a
  *prior* lesson, encoded as the matching atom in this lesson's
  opener — the architect doesn't know the prior lesson, but can
  note an assumed prerequisite by making the first atom a
  recap-style explanation).
- Middle atoms: 1–2 prerequisites each. Synthesis atoms (the ones
  that combine two strands) list both parent atoms.
- Quiz atom: lists every middle atom. A child being quizzed at
  transfer-level has to have seen every strand the distractor probes.

**Engagement / learning-value scoring for age 8**

- Narrative or comparison opener: engagement 0.75–0.9, learning-value
  0.55–0.7.
- Explanation middle: engagement 0.6–0.8, learning-value 0.8–0.95
  (the core-concept atoms should be the highest-learning-value atoms
  in the lesson).
- Cause-effect atom: engagement 0.75–0.85, learning-value 0.85–0.95
  (the *why* is always high-value).
- Experiment penultimate: engagement 0.85–0.95, learning-value
  0.8–0.9.
- Voice (if used): engagement 0.8, learning-value 0.75 — voice is
  a check, not a foundation.
- Final quiz: engagement 0.7, learning-value 0.9.

**Card-type diversity at age 8**

- At least 3 distinct card types across the lesson; 4 is preferred
  at hard difficulty. A 5-atom lesson that is {story, concept,
  concept, concept, quiz} is underperforming — work an experiment
  or a voice card into it.

**Synthesis atoms**

- An age-8 lesson can include one **synthesis** atom: an
  `explanation` or `cause_effect` atom that lists two earlier
  atoms as prerequisites and states the combined claim.
- *Example*: atom-2 teaches "leaves make food", atom-3 teaches
  "roots drink water". Atom-4 synthesises: "leaves and roots work
  together — the water from the roots travels up the stem to the
  leaves, where it meets sunlight to make the plant's food."
- Synthesis atoms are where age-8 lessons earn their complexity.
  Don't skip them.

**Never at age 8**

- More than one voice atom per lesson.
- A cause-effect atom with no prior explanation atom that named
  the cause *and* the effect separately. Cause-effect is synthesis,
  and synthesis needs parts.
- More than 6 atoms — even an eight-year-old's attention has a
  ceiling in this format.
