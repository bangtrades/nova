**Distractor design — build wrong answers that teach.**

A good distractor is one a child who *almost* understands the concept
would actually pick. A throwaway distractor ("apples", when the topic
is gravity) wastes the question. Every wrong option is a chance to
diagnose *which* misconception the child is carrying.

## Canonical misconception patterns by concept type

### vocabulary
- **Phonetic neighbor** — sounds similar but means something else.
- **Category sibling** — a word in the same category that doesn't fit
  the specific meaning (confusing "bark" the sound with "bark" the
  tree covering).
- **Over-general meaning** — a looser word that partially overlaps.

### abstract
- **Concrete-pinning** — treats the abstraction as a specific object.
- **Wrong-level** — applies the concept at a higher or lower level
  than intended.
- **Emotion-mismatch** — attributes a feeling-value to the concept
  that isn't inherent to it.

### process
- **Step skipped** — omits an intermediate step that matters.
- **Step reversed** — gets the order wrong.
- **End-state for middle** — names the final outcome when the
  question asks about a middle step.

### comparison
- **Opposite-direction** — gets the axis right but the direction
  wrong ("heavier" instead of "lighter").
- **Wrong axis** — compares along a different dimension than asked.
- **Equality-when-different** — asserts things are the same when
  they differ in a salient way.

### causeEffect
- **Reversed arrow** — swaps cause for effect.
- **Correlation-for-cause** — names something that co-occurs but
  doesn't cause.
- **Partial cause** — names a contributing factor as the sole cause.

### factual
- **Plausible neighbor** — a fact from the same domain that isn't
  the one asked about.
- **Former-truth** — a fact that used to be true (an outdated count
  of planets, say).
- **Confused-scope** — true at the wrong scale (true for one species
  but not the family).

## Do not use

- Joke options. A child reads them seriously, and the joke becomes a
  misconception later.
- Pop-culture references. They age poorly and cut across cultures.
- Options that require knowledge *outside* the concept being tested.
- Options identical in meaning but reworded — they don't diagnose
  anything, they just crowd the screen.
- "Tricky" options that test reading carefully rather than
  understanding. If the child reads fast and picks the wrong option
  because of a single word, your distractor is doing the wrong job.
